                                         strong seasonal fluctuations significantly alter the structure of the network. On
                                         the other hand, in the Arctic, unlike in more temperate climates, the changing
                                         seasons also bring new opportunities, such as ice roads. Traditional frameworks of
                                         service accessibility assessment, however, rarely take into account such climate-
                                         induced network reconfiguration. To study this phenomenon in detail, we propose
                                         an Environment-Framed Network (EFN) model for analyzing seasonal resilience of
                                         service accessibility in Arctic settlement systems under climate-constrained transport
                                         connectivity. EFN represents transport as a temperature-dependent backbone
                                         and models network layers as service-specific population flows, enabling explicit
                                         treatment of environment-driven link existence. Simulations across four Arctic regions
                                         reveal threshold-driven seasonal reconfigurations: up to 40% of settlements switch
                                         service providers, and 15–20% become temporarily isolated for up to 2–4 months
                                         during seasonal transitions. Despite strong regional heterogeneity, these dynamics
                                         are consistent and governed by the instability of seasonal transport modes. The
                                         results show that resilience in climate-dependent regions is determined primarily by
                                         the stability of service catchments, rather than by static network connectivity.

                  Settlement agglomerations/systems management challenges
                  The accessibility of transportation in the Arctic is affected by a broad set of climatic
                  parameters, among which the most significant are surface air temperature, permafrost
                  condition, and ice cover dynamics (Dong et al. 2025; Waite et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2020).
                  The increase in average annual temperatures leads to a reduction in the period during
                  which the construction and operation of winter roads is possible. Studies show that this
                  process is faster in North America than in Eurasia, where colder winters still allow ice
                  crossings to remain longer (Dong et al. 2025; Povoroznyuk et al. 2022). However, even in
                  the Eurasian part of the Arctic, there is a steady trend towards a decrease in the number
                  of days suitable for the use of such roads (Dong et al. 2025; Waite et al. 2023; Povoro-
                  znyuk et al. 2022). The transportation network plays a key role in ensuring that peo-
                  ple have access to essential services. However, the road network often provides uneven
                  levels of accessibility for different communities. In addition, the accessibility of critical
                  facilities is constantly interrupted by emergencies (Gangwal et al. 2023).
                    A particular threat is the degradation of permafrost, which serves as a natural base
                  for many infrastructure facilities. Warming leads to an increase in the depth of seasonal
                  thawing, which involves ground subsidence, road bed failure, and runway deformation
                  (Waite et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2020; Mahmoud et al. 2024). These processes require sig-
                  nificant repair and modernization costs, which is especially problematic in remote and
                  sparsely populated regions with limited financial resources (Stephenson et al. 2011;
                  Waite et al. 2023; Coates and Broderstad 2019).
                    One of the key conclusions of the reviewed studies is the dual impact of climate change
                  on the Arctic transportation system. On the one hand, the reduction of sea ice opens
                  up new opportunities for shipping (Stephenson et al. 2011; Mahmoud et al. 2024). On
                  the other hand, land-based transportation infrastructure is facing increasing challenges.
                  Winter roads, which traditionally served as the main means of communication between
                  remote settlements during the cold season, are becoming less reliable due to the shorter
                  period of sustained frost (Dong et al. 2025; Stephenson et al. 2011; Coates and Broder-
                  stad 2019). As a result, many regions may face increased transport isolation, especially in
                  the spring and fall periods, when the use of both conventional and winter roads becomes
                  impossible (Dong et al. 2025; Waite et al. 2023).

                  Settlement systems management technologies
                  In general, modern research in the field of urban sustainability shows significant prog-
                  ress in the development of methodological approaches and indicator systems to assess
                  the sustainability of the transportation system. The reviewed studies have evolved from
                  basic statistical methods to advanced hybrid algorithms. In addition to considering the
                  effect of temperature, some studies consider temporal changes. The study (Huang et al.
                  2022) examines spatial and temporal changes in the sustainability of ecological networks
                  (EN), focusing on the landscape and the dynamics of the ecosystem in the evaluations.
                  It uses chain theory to map the migration paths of organisms and identify barriers to the
                  corridor, as in the study (Wang et al. 2023). In (Lu et al. 2022), genetic algorithms are
Kontsevik et al. Applied Network Science   (2026) 11:35                                                          Page 3 of 26




                  combined with neural networks, enabling optimal solutions under high uncertainty typi-
                  cal of urban systems. Paper (Sun et al. 2024) introduces the CRITIC entropy method,
                  which balances indicator significance by contrast and information richness, reducing
                  subjective bias in weight determination. The network method is one of the most appro-
                  priate and frequently used approaches for systematically assessing the availability of
                  critical facilities (Gangwal et al. 2023). By looking at a set of social indicators, such as
                  membership of different communities or household composition, the authors exam-
                  ine the effects of flooding. They used a 100-year flood map to determine which roads
                  would be flooded. This approach is also relevant to the Arctic, where predicting long-
                  term changes is critical to the operation and planning of transportation infrastructure.
                  However, digitalization and environmental analytics remain missing (Huang et al. 2022;
                  Wang et al. 2023; Lu et al. 2022; Ji et al. 2024).

                  Problem statement
                  The most significant gap in the analyzed works is the insufficient consideration of cli-
                  matic features and their impact on urban systems in the long term. For example, the
                  indicators of transport accessibility, discussed in detail in (Yin et al. 2023), do not take
                  into account that in winter many roads in northern regions become difficult to pass or
                  are closed altogether. Similarly, the environmental indicators from (Wang et al. 2023) are
                  not adapted to assess the impacts of extreme weather events, which are becoming more
                  frequent due to climate change. In addition, because of significant climate change, the
                  Arctic requires consideration of impacts in a few decades, as design solutions for north-
                  ern roads should be based on projections of changes in permafrost conditions for at least
                  50 years after construction (Povoroznyuk et al. 2022).
                    Current urban resilience research provides powerful analytical tools but needs signifi-
                  cant refinement to account for temporal and climatic factors. Special attention should
                  be paid to Arctic territories, where traditional resilience assessment methods are often
                  inadequate due to extreme climatic conditions (Coates and Broderstad 2019). Special-
                  ized approaches are required here, taking into account such factors as permafrost, polar
                  nights and extremely low temperatures (Lowe and Sharp 2021).

                  Hypotheses
                  This study develops an Environment-Framed Network (EFN) model to test the following
                  hypotheses:

                    • 	 H1: Remote urban agglomerations exposed to climatic forcing exhibit similar
                        changes in seasonal accessibility patterns.
                    • 	 H2: Seasonal climatic transitions induce systematic service-provider switching and
                        temporary service unavailability.

                  Unlike classical multilayer transport networks, EFN treats transport modes as a shared,
                  environment-constrained backbone and defines layers as service-specific flows over that
                  backbone.

                  Related works
                  In this study, the terms urban agglomerations, settlement systems, and networks of set-
                  tlements are used interchangeably to denote a functionally connected set of cities and
                  rural settlements linked by shared transport infrastructure and service accessibility
                  relations.

                  Network resilience
                  Overall, network resilience theory provides a unified perspective for complex systems in
                  biology, infrastructure, and socioecology. Its core ideas—phase transitions in intercon-
                  nected systems, recovery based on centrality, and multilevel vulnerabilities—offer prac-
                  tical principles for improving resilience.
                    Network resilience has been studied in various fields and research areas, from climate
                  to transportation. Examples of some of these studies include climate networks (e.g.,
                  moisture recycling in the Amazon), which demonstrate feedforward loops that accel-
                  erate cascading transitions. Several models have been proposed for analyzing network
                  resilience. Regional resilience networks, such as China’s economic zones, use modified
                  gravity models to quantify resource flows. Cities with high interconnectedness recover
                  faster but face higher risks of cascading effects during financial crises (Wang et al. 2023).

                  Urban and agglomeration structural stability
                  In recent years, the resilience of urban and agglomeration (or regional) networks has
                  been widely studied (Jie et al. 2024). Research shows that both individual settlements
                  and the entire network of cities suffer simultaneously, experiencing cascading failures
                  as a result of disruptions. The losses incurred can be viewed from an economic, func-
                  tional, or topological perspective (Yin et al. 2023). The phenomenon of urban resilience
                  is examined through specific areas, such as economic or infrastructural urban resilience
                  (which, in addition to other parameters such as road density, includes key urban services
                  such as hospitals) (Guo et al. 2024); and also link it to areas of organizational sustain-
                  ability (market, society) and urban network sustainability (population network, inno-
                  vation network, ecological network) (Mu et al. 2022; Huang et al. 2022), which can be
                  analyzed separately or averaged as a combined measure of urban sustainability (Ji et al.
                  2024). They also highlight the limitations of agglomeration-level research in terms of
                  urban network sustainability, which is important given the interdependence of nodes in
                  the city network.

                  Urban resilience measures
                  (Jie et al. 2024) lists a set of parameters used in research, mainly to assess the resilience
                  and reliability of the urban environment. Other works also include indicators of vul-
                  nerability and anti-fragility (Kiparisov and Lagutov 2024). To assess the resilience of a
                  system, the authors mention the level of hierarchy and assortativity coefficient, degree
                  distribution, and degree correlation. Reliability is explained by the level of structural
                  redundancy (density, average number of independent paths, network structure entropy),
                  modularity, hierarchy, and transmission indicators. In another line of research, the resil-
                  ience of cities (in the context of disruptive events such as natural disasters) is viewed
                  as the resilience of communities to the nonlinearity of complex urban dynamics, with
                  resourcefulness added as an element of resilience. The study of urban subsystems adds a
Kontsevik et al. Applied Network Science   (2026) 11:35                                                            Page 5 of 26




                  quantitative assessment of resourcefulness to weigh the aforementioned characteristics
                  against other indicators (e.g., considering not only the number of base stations but also
                  internet speed) (Yin and Mostafavi 2023).

                  Temporal connectivity resilience of network of cities
                  Cities are connected by transport networks not only by roads, but also by air and water
                  transport. As shown earlier, connectivity, as reflected by various network attributes, is
                  crucial for the stability of network functioning. Although changes in urban networks are
                  often viewed as rapid changes in their properties as a result of specific events, such as
                  natural disasters, traffic accidents, etc. (Yin et al. 2023). Another example of long-term
                  changes is the spatiotemporal evolution of the network and its resilience properties, as
                  assessed for an ecological network (EN) in (Huang et al. 2022). Changes in EN param-
                  eters, including source and corridor (edge) quality, were studied at 8-10 year intervals
                  from 2000 to 2018, revealing significant changes in the variables under study.

                  Distinction of the proposed model (EFN)
                  The layers are service flows rather than transport subnetworks. Unlike most works on
                  multilayer transport networks, where the layers are different transport subsystems (avia-
                  tion, rail, road, water transport, and their connectivity and sustainability; see, e.g. (Li
                  et al. 2025; Mei et al. 2025; Ye et al. 2025)), in EFN, layers are defined by flows of move-
                  ment between cities in terms of a specific service. Settlements have different sets of
                  services and attract populations from settlements that do not have these services in dif-
                  ferent ways. Thus, the set of network layers is formed precisely as a set of flows to differ-
                  ent types of services, rather than as a set of infrastructure subsystems. This brings EFN
                  closer to the literature on trip distribution and the assessment of social facility provision
                  (Mishina et al. 2024), but shifts the focus to a seasonally changing, climate-constrained
                  network of connections.
                    In EFN, transport links are not layers of the model; they are represented as edges of a
                  single backbone network that can belong to different modalities (land, water, air) and be
                  subject to different constraints. Traditional network resilience studies often analyze the
                  importance of nodes (hubs) and the multi-layer effects of their damage (Mei et al. 2025;
                  Ye et al. 2025), but in EFN, the nodes are real settlements, for which short-term “destruc-
                  tion” as a reaction to external disturbances is uncharacteristic and can only be consid-
                  ered in the long term (population outflow, closure of production facilities).
                    At the same time, flows pass along edges — transport links of different modalities,
                  which are affected by weather and climate factors. Changes in temperature and weather
                  conditions can simultaneously affect several modes of transport, as discussed in detail in
                  systematic reviews on weather disruptions in transport and logistics (Touloumidis et al.
                  2025), on the resilience of bus networks in cold regions (Gu et al. 2025), the adaptation
                  of the aviation sector to climate change (Burbidge et al. 2024), and summaries of climate
                  risks and their consideration in infrastructure planning (Rahman et al. 2025; Food 2010).
                  In EFN, it is precisely these climate-dependent changes in the state of the edges that
                  lead to a redistribution of flows and, ultimately, to changes in the level of accessibility of
                  social infrastructure in large cities in the agglomeration.
                    EFN dynamics manifest themselves in seasonal changes in transport connections
                  depending on limiting factors (temperature, weather conditions). The evolution of the
Kontsevik et al. Applied Network Science   (2026) 11:35                                                           Page 6 of 26




                  network occurs due to (i) changes in the set of edges (their existence/non-existence) and
                  (ii) the redistribution of demand between nodes. Layers, interpreted as population flows
                  to specific services, are independent in the current setting and do not generate cross-
                  level cascading effects. This distinguishes EFN from works that explicitly model cascad-
                  ing failures in multilayer transport networks (e.g., in metro-bus dual networks under the
                  influence of temporarily changing passenger flows (Guo et al. 2025; Rouhana and Jawad
                  2025), or in inter-level road networks with cross-layer route selection (Ye et al. 2025).
                     The authors agree that differentiating the consequences of failure of nodes with dif-
                  ferent structural significance (for example, a regional center versus a peripheral village)
                  is an extremely important aspect of network stability analysis. In this paper, we focus
                  on another systemic effect — the dynamics of the redistribution of availability flows
                  between multiple nodes in conditions of seasonal disturbances, where the “disappear-
                  ance” of any settlement simulates the loss of a local service delivery center. The col-
                  lapse of a large transport and logistics hub would have catastrophic consequences that
                  go beyond the seasonal dynamics under consideration and lead, among other things, to
                  long-term migrations and the desolation of dependent territories. Such node-based dis-
                  ruptions are not the focus of this work.
                     The study of the resilience of precisely such supporting, highly centralized nodes to
                  extreme climatic or infrastructural disruptions requires separate, more detailed model-
                  ing with different initial assumptions and is the subject of our further research program.
                     However, as the results show, when considering the dynamics of flow distribution in
                  EFN in a comprehensive manner, the areas of interaction between cities change: areas
                  of attraction to different centers are reformatted, which allows analyzing the seasonal
                  evolution of “service areas” depending on the state of the environment and the network.
                     EFN emphasizes the importance of meeting demand for services, as access to them
                  directly affects quality of life, especially in remote settlements where the consequences
                  of insufficient accessibility are most critical. Here, EFN develops a line of work on assess-
                  ing the provision and accessibility of social infrastructure (Mishina et al. 2024), but con-
                  siders it in the context of explicitly modeled external constraints (climate, seasonality),
                  embedding it in the broader context of climate-oriented solutions for infrastructure and
                  supply chains (Rahman et al. 2025).

                  Transport modes and environmental constraints in the Arctic
                  The transport infrastructure in Arctic regions around the world is characterized by a
                  linear nodal structure with a low network density (Dankin et al. 2022). This is due to
                  extreme climatic conditions, low population density and the complexity of construction
                  in permafrost conditions. Most territories do not have year-round transport links, and
                  the available modes of transport (mainly aviation and water) are characterized by high
                  operating costs. At the same time, there are significant differences in transport accessi-
                  bility between more developed coastal areas and remote inland territories.

                  Arctic unique challenges
                  The Arctic regions are a unique and extremely complex object for network analysis due
                  to the combination of extreme natural, infrastructural, and socio-economic factors that
                  form a fundamentally different logic of transport links compared to temperate latitudes.
                    Firstly, classical static networks (highways, railways) are fragmented or absent here,
                  giving way to dynamic, seasonal and informal communication routes: winter roads that
                  exist only a few months a year; temporary ice crossings; snowmobile trails, the routes
                  of which vary depending on weather conditions. Secondly, multimodality acquires not
                  an additional, but a mandatory and life-supporting character, where the same route
                  between two points can sequentially include moving by car through a winter road,
                  reloading onto a boat to overcome a water barrier and ending with a helicopter section.
                  Thirdly, data on such links are often unsystematic, local in nature, or exist only in the
                  form of oral practice, which makes traditional sources (such as OpenStreetMap (Haklay
                  and Weber 2008)) incomplete and requiring complex verification.
                    Finally, the spatial organization of Arctic communities often follows the logic of access
                  to resources (deer pastures, fishing grounds, oil and gas fields) rather than the princi-
                  ples of economic optimization, creating network structures poorly described by models
                  developed for urbanized territories. Thus, the network model for the Arctic is not a fixed
                  infrastructure, but a dynamic, multimodal and contextually dependent system, the anal-
                  ysis of which requires special methodological adaptations to account for its fundamental
                  instability and weak formalization.

                  Modes and seasonal feasibility

                    • 	 Road transport is key in winter when rivers freeze into seasonal ice roads. However,
                        it fails in deep frost (below −50◦ C) and spring thaw.
                    • 	 Small aircraft ensure year-round access in isolated areas but are vulnerable to icing,
                        storms, and low visibility.
                    • 	 Water transport dominates in summer, but is limited by the short navigation season
                        (2–4 months), ice coverage, and storm threats.
                    • 	 Specialized vehicles (snowmobiles, all-terrain) are widely used by indigenous
                        groups and industry. However, permafrost melting affects terrain passability.
                    • 	 Railways are almost absent due to extreme construction and maintenance costs.

                  Common Arctic transport challenges
                  The transport systems of all Arctic regions of the world face a common set of challenges
                  caused by extreme natural conditions (Dankin et al. 2022; Serova and Serova 2019). All
                  the northern territories are characterized by:

                    • 	 Seasonality — many links are only viable for part of the year (e.g., winter roads,
                        water routes).
                    • 	 High weather dependence — strongly affecting reliability and planning.
                    • 	 Linear, disconnected networks — settlements link to regional hubs, but not to each
                        other.
                    • 	 Multimodality — transport switching between seasons is mandatory.
                    • 	 Extremely high costs — infrastructure and operation often exceed 5–10 times
                        temperate costs.

                  These conditions are found across the Arctic. For example:

                    • 	 Scandinavia has the most robust road network, enhanced by ferry links.
                    • 	 Canada, Russia, USA depend on aircraft and seasonal roads.
                    • 	 Greenland lacks road network, fully dependent on sea and air.

                  Data limitations
                  The key data limitation of this study, which also touches the topic of reuse of the pro-
                  posed method is a scarcity of input data. Being well established in large cities, open
                  transport data is very limited in remote regions, especially for small remote settlements.
                  Another important topic which needs to be covered is a network parameters. Usually in
                  network science the network disruption is considered as a situation when the network
                  disassembles to the extent then one or more nodes stop being connected with the rest of
                  the network. However, based on conducted experiments, for the network of Arctic cities
                  in some areas this is a common situation. Thus in such cases we consider the change in
                  number of months while certain city dose not have an access (in a specified time con-

                  Data schema: graph


                  Network
                  Networks themselves are represented as groups of connected cities in an Arctic region,
                  connected by different kinds of transport modes. Initial state of the network is a cold
                  period (which is most probable period for the Arctic region), dated back to the early
                  1980-s, the beginning of measured temperature time period. The states of the network
                  evolves monthly until 2025th. Thus, each year the Network is represented by 4 seasons
                  of different durations. However the Network states are considered not by seasons but
                  through the limit temperatures, which exceed the threshold of the existence of certain
                  transport mode. Initially every network doesn’t have islands, has a few clear hubs and in
                  most cases is represented as a MultiGraph data class.

                  Edges
                  In this model links are simple connections among cities, representing time (or distance)
                  among two geographical points. Graph of urban agglomeration might have several edges
                  among the same nodes since there are more that one transport option to reach one city
                  from another (such as private car, boat, plane). Each link has transport mode and time
                  as a weight of the edge. However it is important to note that in such remote area as an
                  Arctic region, there is a seasonal specific regarding routes for warm, cold and middle
                  seasons. Thus, there are a few additional types of transport modes, representing the exis-
                  tence of the link. Basically, the private car mode splits into two specific periods (warm
                  and cold), apart from the ones existing through all the seasons. In the midseason it is
                  considered that non of the latter exist. Warm period roads represent less popular routes
                  that exist only in good environmental condition due to the difficulty maintaining them
                  through the year. The roads of the cold period exist in specific cold temperatures. When
                  there is enough snow, it is compressed via specific machines to create road-like surface
                  out of the snow. Such routes are unique and might not repeat the existing warm period
                  routes, but create new edges, connecting cities only in this time of the year.

                  Nodes
                  In this work, nodes represent towns and villages. Each settlement has a set of following
                  parameters: population, number of services of selected types, list of transport facilities
                  such as marinas or airports.

                  Modeling
                  Model proposed in the article
                  In this article we study the network of cities in areas, where the seasonal influence of
                  climatic changes is strong and affects the connectivity between cities. To model and
                  predict evolving seasonal patterns, we present the environment-framed network model
                  (EFN). EFN model has two parameters: the global external parameter of the environ-
                  ment temperature T, the internal set of parameters of the model such as the graph con-
                  nectivity parameter for a graph G(t), which spans the nodes (cities). The model allows to
                  describe how the connectivity of G(t) depends on the environmental parameters, such as
                  temperature.
                    We test the EFN model on the resilience properties looking at: deletion of OD pairs
                  based on the connection probability threshold (Berthelot et al. 2020); stability of the
                  network based on the fulfilled demand for selected services (e.g. clinic in desired time
                  accessibility).

                  Environment-framed (temporal) network model
                  In the EFN framework, the transport network topology evolves through temperature-
                  dependent edge feasibility, while service accessibility evolves through reassignment of
                  OD flows over the feasible network. As a result, temporal changes are analyzed at two
                  coupled levels: (i) network connectivity and (ii) provider-specific service catchments
                  derived from flow assignments (Fig. 2).
                    We consider a dynamic graph G(t) = (V, E(t)), where edges exist probabilistically
                  depending on time and temperature.

                  Regional regime comparison
                  The considered regions differ by location, spatial distribution and density of land, admin-
                  istrative borders, and the amount of big cities with close access 3. Although being in an
                  Arctic region they show different state transition patterns.
                    Across all four regions (Mezen, NAO, Yamalo–Nenets AD, Yakutia/Chukotka), acces-
                  sibility dynamics follow a common seasonal regime characterized by: sharp degradation
                  during spring and autumn transition months, partial stabilization during cold and warm
                  plateaus, recovery to structurally similar states after transitions.
                    Synchronized drops in Jaccard similarity during May–June and September–Octo-
                  ber consistent timing of peak isolation and provider switching months across regions,
                  repeated emergence–disappearance cycles of service catchments rather than monotonic
                  degradation.
                    At the same time, regional topology modulates severity, not mechanism. For example,
                  Yakutia/Chukotka exhibits longer isolation persistence due to sparse topology and win-
                  ter-road dependence, while NAO shows shorter but sharper switching episodes linked
                  to aviation–water substitution. This confirms mechanistic universality with structural
                  heterogeneity, directly supporting the first hypothesis.
Kontsevik et al. Applied Network Science   (2026) 11:35                                                          Page 17 of 26




                    The results show that seasonal transitions are not marginal perturbations but system-
                  level reconfiguration events: switching and isolation peaks are temporally aligned with
                  temperature threshold crossings, not random failures. It is demonstrated that provider
                  switching is driven by loss of feasibility of entire transport modes, forcing reassignment
                  of OD flows even when settlements themselves remain intact.

                  Discussion
                  Limitations

                    • 	 Data availability Although the authors provide the data used in this study, a
                        significant part of it was collected manually. The issue is that exact plane and ship
                        routes on the local scale are usually missing from worldwide datasets.
                    • 	 Simplifications made In this research we do not consider environment-based
                        effects that take place in such region as an Arctic (such as snow storms), that affect
                        the transport routes. These effects are considered as part of temperature restrictions
                        for different transport modes mentioned in the paper.
                    • 	 Temperature thresholds To show rapid changes in transport mode availability due
                        to temperature changes the sigmoid function was used. The threshold value was set
                        to the mean of the sigmoid function to show the transition between seasons. The
                        threshold and decay function could be adjusted for other cases.

                  Conclusion
                  As climate change and globalization amplify systemic risks, these frameworks will be
                  indispensable for designing failsafe infrastructure and sustainable ecosystems.

                  Summary of key findings
                  The EFN model reveals that Arctic transport networks undergo profound seasonal
                  transformations with immediate implications for infrastructure planning and climate
                  adaptation. Revealed uncertain temperature-dependent catchment areas lead to recon-
                  figuration of flows in service-provider relations in the network. Multilayer interactions
                  underline key settlements in the network across several service dimensions.

                  Contributions to the field
                  In real-world applications, nonlinear dynamics in service demand can be observed due
                  to seasonal changes in transport connectivity. As shown in this paper, this effect is most
                  noticeable in a highly climate-dependent region such as the Arctic.
                    This paper contributes to the field in the following ways:

                    • 	 The representation of seasonal patterns in the problem of demand satisfaction.
                        Specifically, with the focus on services at the regional (agglomeration) scale.
                    • 	 Comparative analysis of network dynamics. Seasonal changes in the state of service
                        networks across regions that differ by main types of transport connections, network
                        size and distribution of services (supply) and population (demand).

                  Policy and planning implications
                  The data and results obtained allow us to talk about the necessary development of settle-
                  ments and the definition of their functions. Based on this data, it is possible to draw
Kontsevik et al. Applied Network Science   (2026) 11:35                                                            Page 18 of 26




                  conclusions about maintaining and strengthening transport links, as well as develop-
                  ing infrastructure services in settlements. First and foremost, it is proposed that the
                  results be integrated into the process of developing spatial planning programs for Arctic
                  regions. The method is recommended for use as one of the tools for analyzing trans-
                  port connectivity and the level of infrastructure provision in order to determine strate-
                  gic development goals and compile a list of necessary measures to maintain the system
                  under study:

                    • 	 Identification of priority investment projects, decision-making on the allocation
                        of budget funds, and modernization of infrastructure facilities. This will help focus
                        resources where they will be of greatest benefit to the population.
                    • 	 Development of support programs for the indigenous peoples of the north.
                        Identifying the most vulnerable settlements that need support to preserve their
                        traditional way of life and cultural heritage will ensure their sustainable development.
                    • 	 Evaluating the effectiveness of existing programs.
                    • 	 Optimizing logistics. Improving the logistics of delivering goods and materials to
                        remote areas will reduce costs and increase delivery efficiency.
                    • 	 Developing tourism in remote areas of the north.

                  Future research directions
                  Seasonal transitions between network states in the task of meeting urban service needs
                  form the basis for further research into operations dependent on the external climate
                  environment. In particular, the proposed model is the basis for the use of fundamen-
                  tal models involving (influenced by) the external environment. An example of the use
                  of the presented model could be the optimal placement of services, taking into account
                  changes in demand of a known dependence during a given transition period.
                    Further work on the EFN involves refining the tools and models used. In particular,
                  demand distribution can be specified by more complex behavioral models, including
                  agent-oriented approaches. In addition, the integration of EFN with modern spatio-tem-
                  poral methods of transport flow analysis (Rahman et al. 2025; Li et al. 2024) and climate-
                  oriented risk assessment and infrastructure adaptation approaches (Rahman et al. 2025;
                  Touloumidis et al. 2025; Burbidge et al. 2024) will allow for a more complete description
                  of the response of regional service networks to changing climatic conditions.

                  Appendix A: Manual data collection and data manipulations

                  A.1 Data collection protocols
                  Data was collected and compiled from various sources to build a multimodal transport
                  network and then analyze the effects of temperature. The process of collecting and calcu-
                  lating time metrics for each type of transport was organized as follows: Motor transport.
                  The initial road network was obtained from OpenStreetMap and via OSMnx Python
                  library (Boeing 2025). A weighted graph was built on the basis of this network. The time
                  cost was calculated using shortest path between 2 nodes on a graph, taking into account
                  the actual length of the segment and the assigned speed, differentiated by seasons:
                  60 km/h for the summer period and 40 km/h for the winter period. Settlement polygons
                  were represented as their corresponding centroids; then their centroids were mapped to
                  the closest road graph nodes, to make the calculations possible.
Kontsevik et al. Applied Network Science   (2026) 11:35                                                          Page 19 of 26




                    Aviation transport. Data on scheduled flights and flight times were collected from
                  official sources: local airline schedules, airport websites, and local government regula-
                  tions (Aviotryad 2025; Arktika 2025; Yamal 2025; TSAero 2025; Chukotavia 2025; Taimyr
                  2025). For connected settlements, between which there is no regular communication,
                  or data about it is not given in the schedule, the flight time was estimated based on the
                  geodesic distance, taking into account the typical cruising speed of the most common
                  aircraft, the Mi-8 helicopter.
                    Water transport. Information about current river and sea routes, as well as travel time,
                  was extracted from the websites of water transport operators (Severnaya Transportnaya
                  Kompaniya 2025). The travel time for water transport was calculated based on the length
                  of the fairway and the accepted average operating speed of 20 km/h. This value is justified
                  by the analysis of the fleet of vessels involved on local routes (boats of the “Yaroslavets”
                  and “KS” types), for which the typical cruising speed in conditions of winding rivers with
                  depth restrictions and the need for frequent stops is 20-25 km/h (Korinevsky and Logi-
                  nov 2006). This approach provides a conservative and realistic estimate of time costs.

                  A.2 Validation and evaluation of data quality
                  The initial cartographic data from OpenStreetMap, although valuable, may be incomplete
                  (Deri et al. 2025) or inaccurate for remote and infrastructurally underdeveloped regions,
                  such as the Arctic (Kwakye et al. 2024). To ensure the completeness and reliability of the
                  transport network model, a step-by-step process of data validation and addition was car-
                  ried out.: Cross-validation and addition of the route network. The OSM data was com-
                  pared with alternative cartographic sources (Google 2025). The identified gaps (winter
                  roads, actual roads, and potential ice crossings, etc.) were supplemented based on an
                  analysis of regulations - seasonal resolutions of local authorities on the opening/closing
                  of winter roads and crossings (Republic 2024; Russian 2025; Community 2025), as well as
                  studying current messages in regional social networks reflecting real practices of popula-
                  tion displacement.
                    Connections were evaluated for each type of transport, for which it was possible to
                  obtain the actual time according to the schedule and compare it with the calculated val-
                  ues. This made it possible to characterize the level of detail of the model and outline
                  directions for its future refinement.
                    Expert evaluation and logical verification: The calculated time matrices for all modes
                  of transport have been logically verified for consistency (for example, flight time can-
                  not exceed the time of a similar car trip over significant distances). Where possible, the
                  results were selectively compared with data from navigation services and expert assess-
                  ments. The experts in this case were local residents and their relevant experience of real
                  trips.

                  Appendix B: Comparison logic

                  B.1 Regional comparison
                  Selected territories represent a wide range of settlement conditions, infrastructure facili-
                  ties and transport models. A comparative analysis of key parameters makes it possible to
                  identify systemic differences and common features characteristic of the Arctic as a whole.
                  In total, 9 out of 74 municipalities of the Arctic zone of the Russian Federation were stud-
Kontsevik et al. Applied Network Science   (2026) 11:35                                                            Page 20 of 26




                  ied, which displays 1/4 of the territory of the Russian Arctic (Dankin et al. 2022; Pilyasov
                  and Zamyatina 2016; Council 2023).
                    The comparison reveals a fundamental diversity of spatial and transport models within
                  the considered region: from the relatively connected and infrastructurally developed west-
                  ern periphery (NAO and Mezen Municipal District) through polycentric industrial areas
                  with a river framework (Yamalo-Nenets Autonomous District/Taimyr) to dispersed ter-
                  ritories of the east, extremely dependent on seasonal winter routes (Yakutia/Chukotka).
                  Despite these differences, all territories show common systemic features: The critical role
                  of seasonal and informal transport (winter roads, snowmobile trails) complementing the
                  official network. The formation of transport links across administrative borders, dictated
                  by the logic of the natural environment and economic activity. There is a sharp contrast in
                  accessibility between a few hubs (administrative or industrial centers) and a vast periph-
                  ery. The dependence of the transport framework on a limited number of natural objects
                  (large rivers, coast) and modes of transport (air, winter). Successful adaptation and appli-
                  cation of a unified methodology for territories with such different settlement densities,
                  network topology and socio-economic structure will show its universality.

                  B.2 Service catchments stability thresholds
                  The identification of unstable settlements and the determination of thresholds after
                  which a settlement is considered “disappeared” in a dynamic model is a projection of
                  the vulnerability of the socio-spatial structure of the region. In the context of the Arctic,
                  the disappearance of the settlement signals a critical degradation of its functions in the
                  transport and social system. Settlement stability thresholds correspond to the following
                  real processes:
                     1. Loss of critical transport connectivity. A settlement (or group of settlements) ceases
                  to be a stable integrated element of a multimodal network during the season. This leads to
                  a regime of point isolation of the settlement or to the destruction of local socio-economic
                  contours and undermines the basis for the use of limited infrastructure.
                     2. Cascading effect on neighboring and related settlements. The disappearance of one
                  settlement leads to an increase in the burden on the remaining stable centers, disruption
                  of supply chains and entails long-term socio-demographic reactions (outflow of the most
                  mobile population - youth).
                     3. A systemic shift in the settlement model. The disappearance of a settlement or an
                  entire cluster of settlements reflects the transition of the system to a qualitatively new
                  state. For example, the transition from a polycentric model (several local support vil-
                  lages) to a monocentric one (absolute dependence on one remote regional center). This
                  dramatically increases the vulnerability of the system as a whole — the failure of one main
                  center paralyzes the entire periphery. Because in a situation where one locality becomes
                  isolated from the entire system, its services cease to be available to the population of other
                  settlements, or services outside its borders become inaccessible to its population.
                     Thus, the stability threshold we have set is an indicator of the point at which the deg-
                  radation of transport accessibility ceases to be an operational problem and becomes a
                  threat to the sustainability of the settlement itself as a social unit. This approach makes
                  it possible to use the network model not only to describe the current state, but also to
                  proactively identify “points of failure” in the Arctic settlement system.

                  Appendix C: Urban policy applications

                  C.1 Extended discussion
                  The probabilistic temperature-dependent multilayer network model (EFN) developed in
                  the study allowed us to move from qualitative descriptions to a quantitative analysis of
                  the seasonal dynamics of transport accessibility in the Arctic. The results obtained reveal
                  the systemic properties of Arctic infrastructure networks and suggest new optics for their
                  planning.
                    Interpretation of key results. Our results show that the external instability hides highly
                  structured patterns of seasonal variability in the system configuration.
                    1. Non-linearity and threshold effect. The model confirmed that the availability of ser-
                  vices does not change smoothly, but rather abruptly during periods of seasonal transi-
                  tions (melting/ice formation) (Ahmed 2005).
                    The threshold character set by the sigmoid function adequately reflects the reality when
                  a slight warming above the critical point leads to the collapse of the entire network of
                  winter roads, rather than their gradual deterioration.
                    2. Vulnerability of polycentricity: An analysis of the evolution of settlements revealed
                  that the stability of the system depends not on individual roads, but on the stability of
                  local centers of attraction (settlement nodes). The disappearance of such a settlement in
                  the model (the “death” event) means not just the isolation of several settlements, but the
                  disintegration of the local cluster structure, which forces the remaining settlements to
                  reorient to more remote and congested centers. This explains why up to 40% of localities
                  are forced to change service providers: the system is constantly being rebuilt, trying to
                  find a new balance after the loss of local node and new service providers.
                    3. Geographical universality of mechanisms with local diversity: Despite the radical
                  differences between the regions (linear structure of the Northern Administrative District
                  vs. polycentric Yamalo-Nenets Autonomous District vs. dispersed Yakutia/Chukotka),
                  the basic mechanisms of degradation and restoration of connectivity turned out to be
                  common. In all cases, seasonal modes of transport (winter roads, river routes) play a key
                  role, and shifts in accessibility are synchronized with temperature cycles. This proves that
                  the proposed EFN model is precisely the generalizing framework applicable to different
                  configurations of Arctic settlement systems. Methodology and limits of generalization.
                  The EFN model contributes by allowing for what has traditionally been left outside the
                  scope of analysis. From static to dynamic: Unlike static infrastructure maps, EFN simu-
                  lates network performance over time, which is critical for regions where the road exists
                  only 4 months a year.
                    Integration of the human factor and informal practices: Manual collection of data on
                  unofficial winter roads and local routes made it possible to include real, not only official,
                  travel practices in the model. This increases the adequacy of the model, but at the same
                  time points to its main limitation — dependence on time-consuming collection of expert
                  data. The development of automated methods (track analysis, social media) is a key area
                  for scaling.
                    Consideration of extreme weather events (blizzards, storms) as part of the general-
                  ized temperature threshold, this is a necessary simplification at the current stage. Tacti-
                  cal planning will require embedding probabilistic models of specific hazards that disrupt
                  specific relationships.
Kontsevik et al. Applied Network Science   (2026) 11:35                                                           Page 22 of 26




                  Fig. 13 Network scheme

                    Accounting for extreme weather events through an aggregated temperature threshold
                  is a strategic simplification adopted in this paper to achieve the main goal of modeling
                  long—term seasonal dynamics. This simplification is based on two key assumptions:
                    Correlation with seasonality: Peaks in the activity of the main disrupting factors (snow-
                  storms leading to the closure of winter shelters; storms stopping river and sea navigation)
                  are statistically timed to the periods of seasonal transitions, which in the model are deter-
                  mined by temperature thresholds.
                    Integral effect: On a monthly or seasonal scale, the cumulative effect of multiple short-
                  term extreme events manifests itself as an extension of the period of “unavailability” of a
                  particular type of transport, which is recorded by the model through a shift in the thresh-
                  old of its operation.

                  C.2 Formation of a new spatial planning logic
                  The results obtained are changing the approach to infrastructure management in the
                  Arctic.

                    1. Prioritization of investments: The model allows us to shift the focus from repairing
                       specific road sections to strategically strengthening the backbone links that keep
                       entire communities from disintegrating. Investments in the capitalization of a key
                       winter mine leading to a vulnerable cluster will have a greater systemic effect than
                       repairing several disparate sites.
                    2. Adaptive spatial planning: Identified “accessibility windows” and seasonal patterns
                       of flow redistribution should form the basis for adaptive work schedules for social
                       services, educational and medical teams. Planning of the northern delivery should
                       also take into account not just the navigation period, but the dynamically changing
                       map of accessibility of settlements.
                    3. Proactive social risk management: The fact of seasonal isolation of 15-20% of
                       settlements is not only a transport problem, but also an acute social problem. The EFN
                       model makes it possible to predict these periods and develop proactive measures: the
                       creation of food and medicine supplies, the organization of temporary medical care
                       centers, distance education and consultation schemes (Fig. 13).

                  D.2 Temperature model
                  Let T (x, t) denote the temperature field at geographic location x and month t. For each
                  settlement i with coordinates xi , temperature is sampled as

                           Ti (t) := T (xi , t).

                  Temperature values are used solely to evaluate operational feasibility constraints of
                  transport modes and do not represent disruption, degradation, or any internal state of
                  the settlement nodes.
                    Because the model operates on an origin–destination abstraction rather than explicit
                  route geometries, we define an effective temperature proxy for each OD pair (i, j) as
                                       (              )
                           T̃ij (t) = g Ti (t), Tj (t) ,

                  where g(·) aggregates endpoint temperatures. In the baseline specification, g is defined
                  as the arithmetic mean of endpoint temperatures; alternative conservative aggregations
                  can be used to reflect stricter feasibility assumptions.
                    Seasonal effects are not introduced as an explicit categorical variable. Instead, season-
                  ality emerges implicitly through the temporal evolution of Ti (t) and its interaction with
                  mode-specific feasibility thresholds, enabling continuous transitions between accessibil-
                  ity regimes.
                    For interpretability, we refer to temperature ranges separated by feasibility thresholds
                  as seasonal phases. Transitions between these phases are triggered by crossings of mode-
                  specific temperature thresholds rather than by predefined calendar seasons.
                    Based on the obtained data, the feasibility of transport mode existence is modeled as a
                  sigmoid function of temperature, reflecting threshold-like operational limits under sea-
                  sonal climate conditions (Fig. 14).

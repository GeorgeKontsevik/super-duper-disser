Title:​
Assessment of Transport Networks Stability in the context of Climate
Stressors on Urban Agriculture Supply Chain in Equatorial regions.

Abstract


Introduction (1–1.5 pages)
Cities across the equatorial belt are expanding at unprecedented rates, and this rapid urban
growth is transforming the way food moves from farms to consumers. Unlike temperate regions,
where climate patterns follow relatively predictable seasonal cycles, equatorial cities must
contend with intense rainfall, high temperatures, rising flood risks, and year-round agricultural
activity. As a result, food systems in these regions depend heavily on transportation networks
that operate under near-constant environmental stress. These networks, which include roads,
bridges, and feeder routes from peri-urban farms, function as the “circulatory system” of the
urban food economy. When they fail, disruptions ripple quickly through supply chains and
threaten food access for millions.

Urban agriculture has become an increasingly important buffer for food security in these
fast-growing regions, especially where formal food distribution systems remain underdeveloped.
Yet the success of urban agriculture hinges on the reliability of transport infrastructure. Even
minor disruptions can slow the movement of perishable goods, increase spoilage, and destabilize
market prices. Studies consistently show that the majority of food consumed in low- and
middle-income urban areas is sourced through rural–urban supply chains, making transport
connectivity central to both availability and affordability of food [1]. When climate-related
hazards interrupt these links, the impacts extend well beyond localized damage. Modeling
evidence demonstrates that even isolated network disruptions can generate indirect losses across
entire regions, affecting households and businesses that are not directly exposed to the hazard
[2].

Climate change is intensifying the frequency and severity of disruptive weather events in
equatorial regions. Short, intense rainfall episodes—common in these climates—have been
empirically linked to abrupt declines in road serviceability and transport operations. Projections
indicate that these interruptions will grow more frequent as climate conditions shift [3]. Flooding
remains the most immediate concern, but the risks are compounded in cities where impermeable
land cover, rapid urban expansion, and the urban heat island effect accelerate infrastructure
degradation [4]. Low-lying neighborhoods, which often serve as transit corridors between
peri-urban farms and central markets, face particularly high vulnerability.

Despite growing recognition of these challenges, existing research has not sufficiently examined
the intersection of transport network stability, climate stressors, and urban agriculture. Much of
the literature focuses on export commodities or passenger transport, overlooking the unique
characteristics of urban food supply chains. Food systems in equatorial cities often rely on
informal markets, short delivery cycles, and decentralized production sites—features that alter
how climate hazards propagate through the network.

This study addresses these gaps by assessing the stability of transport networks in equatorial
regions under different climate stress scenarios, with a focus on their role in urban agriculture
supply chains. By combining open-source transport data, climate indicators, and spatial network
models, we identify vulnerable corridors, quantify potential impacts of climate-induced
disruptions, and highlight opportunities for improving resilience. Understanding these dynamics
is crucial for guiding infrastructure investments, strengthening urban food systems, and
protecting vulnerable communities in the face of growing climate uncertainty.


Literature Review
   ●​ 2.1 Urban Agriculture: Concept & Trends

Urban agriculture is a broad concept encompassing a wide variety of food production practices
within cities and surrounding areas, including everything from small-scale home gardens to large
commercial peri-urban farms. Although widely acknowledged in policy and academic circles,
the term lacks a universally accepted definition, as it encompasses a diverse range of practices,
scales, and motivations. For this review, we adopt a functional definition that highlights urban
agriculture’s role in providing food for cities, which includes both intra-urban production and the
peri-urban agricultural zones that serve as vital suppliers to urban markets.

The rapid global trend toward urbanization has significantly altered food system dynamics,
particularly in low- and middle-income countries, where urban growth rates are the highest.
Cities in these regions have become hubs for food consumption, serving as aggregation points
for the production and distribution of goods to a growing urban population. However, this
transformation has made cities increasingly dependent on complex supply chains that connect
rural producers with urban consumers [1]. In equatorial regions, this process is further
complicated by the rising demand for fresh produce and staple goods, creating pressure on
supply chains to source food from increasingly distant agricultural zones.

In Africa, urban and peri-urban agriculture are key components of food security strategies. In
many West African cities, for example, agricultural supply chains remain informal and
vulnerable to the challenges of underdeveloped infrastructure. Studies in Ghana’s Accra
metropolis illustrate how food provisioning relies on a web of wholesalers, transporters, and
market vendors operating with minimal institutional support [5]. Similarly, comparative research
across Ouagadougou (Burkina Faso), Bamako (Mali), and other West African cities reveals how
regional distribution dynamics vary according to factors such as seasonality and product type [6].

In Southeast Asia, rapid urbanization has led to significant pressures on both agricultural
production and transport infrastructure. Cities like Jakarta and Manila, for instance, face dual
challenges of urban sprawl and climate vulnerability, including frequent flooding and the rise of
urban heat islands. These challenges not only threaten agricultural zones but also jeopardize the
transport networks critical for food distribution [4]. In some areas, urban expansion has led to the
conversion of agricultural land into residential and industrial zones, reducing local food
production capacity and increasing dependence on distant rural areas, which amplifies the
vulnerability of transport networks.

In South America, large-scale commodity agriculture dominates; yet, even here, disruptions to
transportation networks can have serious consequences. Research on Brazil’s soybean export
corridors demonstrates how road disruptions result in substantial cost increases, with disruptions
exceeding 10% on key routes during perturbation scenarios [7]. Though this study focuses on
export commodities, it offers valuable insights for urban agriculture supply chains, which
similarly rely on well-maintained and reliable transport routes for the movement of goods.

Emerging Challenges

Across all equatorial regions, the challenges faced by urban agriculture are compounded by
climate change, aging infrastructure, and weak governance. Many urban food systems, especially
in the equatorial areas, operate informally without coordinated policy frameworks or substantial
investment in infrastructure. Informal markets often lack basic facilities, such as cold storage or
loading zones, making them highly susceptible to transport disruptions. These vulnerabilities are
exacerbated by the increasing frequency and severity of extreme weather events, which further
highlight the need for climate-resilient transport infrastructure to support urban food supply
chains [5].

   ●​ 2.2 Supply Chain Impact Factors

The stability and efficiency of urban agriculture supply chains depend on a multitude of factors,
including market accessibility, the quality of road infrastructure, and climate stressors. These
factors do not operate in isolation but instead interact in ways that can amplify vulnerabilities,
particularly in equatorial cities.
Market Accessibility and Infrastructure

Market accessibility includes both physical access to markets via roads and the economic access
to affordable food, which is shaped by transaction costs, market power, and access to
information. In equatorial cities, poor infrastructure and congestion exacerbate the vulnerability
of food systems to transport disruptions. Informal markets, which are prevalent in many African
and Asian cities, are particularly at risk due to their reliance on poorly maintained roads and the
absence of cold storage or other critical facilities [5].

Research in Accra highlights how the lack of formal planning for urban food distribution results
in inefficient market locations, suboptimal routing, and a lack of capacity to respond to
disruptions in the supply chain [5]. The fragmented nature of food supply systems in these cities
means that even a minor disruption can have cascading effects, undermining food access for
vulnerable populations.

Road Infrastructure Quality and Network Criticality

The quality of road networks is a key determinant of supply chain resilience. Critical road
links—such as bridges or narrow streets—can have a disproportionate impact on network
performance, especially when roads are already congested or in disrepair. In equatorial regions,
many roads suffer from chronic underinvestment, making them prone to disruption from extreme
weather events. For example, unpaved rural roads often become impassable during the rainy
season, and urban roads may lack adequate drainage systems, rendering them vulnerable to
flooding [2]. Network criticality analysis identifies the most important roads in a supply chain,
focusing attention on those links that are most essential to the functioning of urban food systems.

Climate Stressors

Climate change introduces a range of stressors that threaten transport network stability,
particularly in equatorial regions. Flooding, both riverine and pluvial, is one of the most
immediate threats, with frequent short-duration, high-intensity rainfall events causing significant
transport disruptions. Studies show that these extreme events will increase in frequency under
climate change scenarios, leading to greater instability in transport networks [3]. The
compounded effects of urban heat islands and land use change create further vulnerabilities,
particularly in low-lying urban areas with poor drainage and high levels of impermeability [4].

Flooding disrupts the movement of goods by blocking roads and limiting access to markets,
while high temperatures accelerate pavement degradation, making roads more susceptible to
flooding damage. These vulnerabilities are compounded by the increasing conversion of
agricultural land to urban areas, which reduces the redundancy of supply routes and increases the
reliance on already stressed transport networks.
Global Case Studies

Several global case studies demonstrate how disruptions to transport infrastructure can
significantly affect food supply chains:

   ●​ Tanzania: The DisruptSCT modeling framework applied to Tanzania shows how
      transport failures can ripple through production and consumption networks, with indirect
      losses affecting areas not directly impacted by the disruption [2]. This case study
      emphasizes how disruptions to key links can trigger a cascade of economic losses,
      illustrating the far-reaching impacts of transport instability.​

   ●​ Brazil: Research on road disruptions in Brazil’s soybean export corridors reveals that
      cost increases exceeding 10% occur on many routes during disruption scenarios [7].
      These findings are highly relevant to urban agriculture, where similar disruptions to key
      transport corridors can lead to significant increases in food prices and disruptions in
      market supply.​

   ●​ West Africa: Empirical studies in West African cities, such as Accra, highlight how
      informal markets and inadequate infrastructure increase vulnerability to transport failures.
      Research in Accra shows that ad hoc market locations and the absence of formal
      city-level food distribution planning contribute to inefficiencies in food supply systems
      [5].



   ●​ 2.3 Transport Network Stability and Resilience

Transport network stability—the ability of a network to maintain functionality under stress—is
fundamental to supply chain resilience. For urban agriculture, where perishable goods must
move quickly from production sites to markets, even brief transport disruptions can result in
spoilage, price volatility, and food access gaps. This section reviews key concepts, methods, and
findings from the literature on transport network resilience, with emphasis on applications
relevant to urban food systems.

Importance of Stability for Supply Chains

Stable road networks maintain frequent, timely flows of staple and perishable goods; when links
fail, indirect economic and food security impacts propagate across regions and actor groups[2].
The importance of stability extends beyond simple connectivity to encompass reliability,
redundancy, and adaptive capacity. Reliability refers to consistent travel times and costs;
redundancy involves alternative routes or modes; and adaptive capacity reflects the ability of
network users and managers to respond to disruptions through rerouting, demand management,
or temporary infrastructure solutions.

For urban agriculture supply chains, stability is particularly critical because:

1. Perishability: Fresh produce has a limited shelf life, making delays costly.

2. Frequency: Many urban markets require daily or near-daily deliveries, offering little buffer for
disruptions.

3. Scale: Small-scale producers and vendors often lack resources to absorb losses from spoilage
or missed market days.

4. Informality: Informal supply chains may lack contractual protections or insurance, placing the
full burden of disruption on the most vulnerable actors.

Key Research Findings

The literature on transport network resilience has developed several analytical approaches that
can inform assessments of urban agriculture supply chains:

-Network Criticality and Input-Output Coupling: The DisruptSCT approach couples network
criticality metrics with input-output and agent-based models to quantify direct and indirect
impacts of transport disruption[2]. Network criticality analysis identifies links or nodes whose
failure would most severely degrade system performance, while input-output modeling traces
how disruptions propagate through economic sectors. Agent-based simulation adds behavioral
realism by modeling how firms and households respond to disruptions. Applied to Tanzania, this
integrated framework revealed that indirect losses from transport failures can exceed direct
infrastructure damage costs by an order of magnitude, with impacts distributed unevenly across
regions and socioeconomic groups[2]

-Complex Network Theory and Flood Scenarios: Resilience assessment frameworks applying
complex network theory together with designed flood inundation scenarios reveal spatial patterns
of vulnerability and dynamic performance loss in coastal urban transport networks [8]. These
studies typically construct networks as graphs (nodes and edges), apply disturbance scenarios
(e.g., inundation at specified depths), and measure performance using metrics such as network
efficiency, connectivity loss, and travel time increases. The approach allows identification of
critical infrastructure and assessment of how network performance degrades under different
flood severities.

-Pluvial Flood Impact Modeling: Scenario-based modeling of pluvial flooding links rainfall
intensity and duration to localized transport operational loss [3]. By simulating drainage system
capacity and road inundation thresholds, these models estimate which road segments will
become impassable under different rainfall scenarios. Coupling these estimates with climate
projections provides forward-looking assessments of how transport disruption frequency and
severity may change. Studies consistently find that short-duration,                high-intensity
events—common in equatorial climates—pose the greatest disruption risk[3].

Vulnerability Assessment Tools

Common tools and metrics identified in the literature include:

- Network criticality analysis: identifying essential links/nodes[2]

- Input-output economic linkage modeling: tracing sectoral impacts[2]

- Agent-based propagation simulation: modeling behavioral responses[2]

- Complex network metrics: efficiency, betweenness centrality, robustness[8]

- Flood inundation scenario design: linking hazard to network performance[3]

- Techno-economic route disruption analysis: quantifying cost impacts[7]

These tools offer complementary perspectives: network analysis focuses on structural properties,
economic modeling traces indirect impacts, and scenario simulation explores dynamic responses.
Integrating multiple approaches provides a more complete picture of resilience.

Research Gaps

Despite advances in transport network resilience research, several gaps remain, particularly
regarding urban agriculture supply chains:

1. Urban Agriculture-Specific Analysis: Most studies focus on general urban mobility,
commuter traffic, or export commodities. The specific characteristics of urban agriculture supply
chains—short distances, high frequency, perishability, informality—have received limited
attention. There is insufficient evidence on how transport disruptions uniquely affect urban food
systems compared to other supply chains.

2. Integrated Assessment of Combined Stressors: Few studies fully integrate land use change,
urban expansion, heat effects, and hydrological extremes in one framework[4,8]. Most analyses
focus on a single stressor (usually flooding), missing potential interactions. For example,
heat-induced pavement degradation may worsen flood damage, or urban expansion may
simultaneously increase flood exposure and reduce network redundancy.

3. Informal Market Dynamics: The role of informal markets in mediating transport disruptions
is poorly understood. Informal systems may exhibit flexibility and adaptive capacity that formal
models miss, or they may be more vulnerable due to a lack of resources and institutional support.
Empirical research highlights the importance of informal markets in urban food systems but
rarely integrates this understanding into quantitative resilience assessments[5,6].
4. Standardized Methodologies: Context and commodity specificity complicate generalization;
a corridor critical for one supply chain may be unimportant for another[2]. There is a need for
standardized methodologies that can be adapted to different contexts while allowing comparison
across studies. Such methods should reflect heterogeneous supply chain roles and policy
objectives, enabling prioritization of investments that address the most critical vulnerabilities.

3. Methods (Materials & Methods)
This section describes the data sources, analytical approach, procedural workflow, and
limitations of the study. The methodology is designed to assess the stability of transport networks
under climate stress scenarios for urban agriculture supply chains in equatorial regions, with an
emphasis on reproducibility and transparency.

3.1 Data Sources and Selection
The study integrates multiple data sources to construct a comprehensive picture of transport
networks, urban agriculture supply chains, and climate stressors.
Transport network data (OpenStreetMap)
OpenStreetMap (OSM) serves as the primary source for transport networks and road
infrastructure. OSM provides open-access, crowd-sourced geographic information on road
networks, including road classifications (primary, secondary, tertiary, residential), surface types
(paved, unpaved), and connectivity [15, 17]. Despite limitations in data completeness and
accuracy in some regions, OSM represents the most comprehensive openly available transport
network dataset for many equatorial cities that lack official GIS infrastructure data [15].
For this study, OSM data are extracted for selected equatorial urban regions using the OSMnx
Python library [15], which facilitates downloading, modeling, and analyzing street networks.
Road segments are classified by type and surface, allowing differentiation between major
arterials and minor roads, and between all‑weather paved roads and more vulnerable unpaved
routes.
Climate indicators
Climate stressor data are obtained from multiple sources:
Historical rainfall data: Precipitation records from meteorological stations and gridded datasets
such as CHIRPS [9] and ERA5 [10] provide historical patterns of rainfall intensity, duration, and
frequency. These data are used to identify areas experiencing extreme rainfall events and to
characterize typical and extreme conditions.
Flood hazard maps: Where available, flood hazard maps from national agencies or global
datasets (e.g., GLOFAS [12] or similar global flood hazard products) identify areas at risk of
riverine or pluvial flooding. These maps are integrated with transport network data to identify
vulnerable road segments [3, 8].
Urban heat and land‑use data: Satellite-derived land surface temperature data (e.g., Landsat [13],
MODIS [14]) and land‑cover classifications identify urban heat islands and areas with high
impermeability. These indicators help assess combined climate vulnerabilities [4].
Urban agriculture and market data
Data on urban agriculture production zones and market locations are compiled from:

   ●​ Published studies and reports documenting food supply chains in study cities [5, 6]
   ●​ Market location data from municipal sources or OSM points of interest [17]

These data are inherently less precise than transport network data, reflecting the informal and
dynamic nature of urban food systems. Where comprehensive data are unavailable, proxy
indicators (e.g., distance from city center, land‑use classification) are used [5, 6].
Geographic scope
The study focuses on selected cities in equatorial regions (broadly defined as latitudes within 15°
of the equator), chosen to represent diverse contexts in terms of city size, economic
development, and climate exposure. Cities are selected based on data availability, relevance to
urban agriculture supply chains, and representation of different continental regions (Africa, Asia,
South America) [1, 4].
Rationale for data selection
The choice of open‑source data (OSM, CHIRPS, ERA5, Landsat/MODIS) prioritizes
reproducibility, accessibility, and applicability to data‑scarce contexts [9, 10, 13, 14, 15]. While
proprietary or official datasets may offer higher accuracy in specific cases, open‑source data
enable replication and extension of the methodology to other cities and regions. The integration
of multiple data types—transport, climate, agricultural—reflects the multi‑dimensional nature of
supply‑chain resilience [2, 3, 8].1

3.2 Analytical Approach
The analytical framework consists of four main steps: network extraction and characterization,
climate indicator integration, vulnerability metrics calculation, and spatial analysis. Each step
employs established methods adapted to the urban agriculture supply‑chain context [2, 3, 8, 15,
16].
Step 1: Network extraction and characterization
Transport networks are extracted from OSM using OSMnx [15], which constructs graph
representations of road networks with nodes (intersections) and edges (road segments). Network
characterization includes:
Classification: Roads are classified by type (primary, secondary, tertiary, residential) and surface
(paved, unpaved) to differentiate infrastructure quality and climate vulnerability [3, 15].
Topological metrics: Standard network metrics—node degree, edge betweenness centrality,
closeness centrality—are calculated using NetworkX [16] to identify structurally important
network elements. Betweenness centrality, which measures the frequency with which a road
segment appears on shortest paths between node pairs, is particularly relevant for identifying
critical corridors [2, 8, 16].
Connectivity analysis: Network connectivity is assessed by measuring the number of alternative
routes between key locations (e.g., production zones and markets). Low connectivity indicates
vulnerability to single‑point failures [2, 8].

Climate stressor data are spatially overlaid with transport networks to identify vulnerable road
segments:
Flood exposure: Flood hazard maps are intersected with road network geometries to identify
segments within flood‑prone areas; segments are tagged with flood depth or probability classes
for scenario analysis [3, 8, 12].
Rainfall intensity: Historical rainfall data are used to calculate exceedance probabilities for
different intensity thresholds. Road segments in areas experiencing frequent high‑intensity events
are flagged as high‑risk [3, 9, 10].
Heat and impermeability: Urban heat and land‑cover data identify areas where combined heat
and flood stressors may compound vulnerability. Road segments in highly impermeable,
low‑lying areas receive elevated vulnerability scores [4, 13, 14].
Temporal patterns: Seasonal and interannual variability in climate stressors are characterized to
understand when vulnerabilities are highest (e.g., rainy season) [9, 10].
Step 3: Vulnerability metrics calculation
Vulnerability metrics combine network structural importance with climate exposure:
Climate‑weighted criticality: Betweenness centrality is weighted by climate exposure (e.g., flood
probability, rainfall intensity) to produce a climate‑weighted criticality score. High scores
indicate road segments that are both structurally important and climate‑exposed [2, 3, 8].
Supply‑chain‑specific metrics: For urban agriculture, vulnerability is assessed relative to specific
origin–destination pairs (production zones to markets), including:

   ●​ Route redundancy (number of alternative routes between production zones and markets);
   ●​ Expected disruption frequency (probability that critical routes become impassable under
      different climate scenarios);
   ●​ Travel time/cost increases when primary routes are disrupted, and traffic is rerouted [2, 7,
      8].

Spatial vulnerability index: A composite index combining multiple indicators (criticality,
exposure, redundancy) is calculated for each road segment and aggregated to identify vulnerable
zones [3, 8].
Step 4: Spatial analysis and visualization
Spatial analysis techniques are used to visualize and interpret vulnerability patterns:
Hotspot analysis: Spatial clustering methods (e.g., Getis–Ord Gi*) identify hotspots where
vulnerable road segments are concentrated.
Supply‑chain pathway analysis: Least‑cost paths between production zones and markets are
mapped on climate‑adjusted networks, with overlays of vulnerability scores to identify at‑risk
supply‑chain corridors [2, 7].
All spatial analysis and visualization are conducted in Python using OSMnx [15], NetworkX
[16], GeoPandas, Matplotlib, and related libraries.

3.2.1 Climate anomalies and road‑status rules
To translate climate anomalies into changes in network performance, the analysis applies simple,
literature‑informed rules to OSM road classes and surfaces. Empirical studies of pluvial flooding
link surface‑water depth and duration to loss of service or closure on urban roads, with
lower‑standard and unpaved roads particularly sensitive to shallow flooding [3]. Based on these
findings and global flood hazard modeling practices [3, 12], the following rules are adopted:
Flood depth:
Road segments intersecting areas with estimated flood depths ≥ 0.30 m are treated as impassable
if they are unpaved residential, tertiary, or track‑type links, reflecting their high sensitivity to
ponding and surface damage [3].
Paved residential and tertiary segments under the same depth are kept in the network but
assigned substantial speed reductions.
Primary and secondary segments are only removed when depths exceed 0.50 m, consistent with
their higher design standards and the tendency of major corridors to remain passable up to
greater depths, albeit at reduced speeds [3, 8].
Rainfall intensity:
Short‑duration, high‑intensity rainfall events are identified using CHIRPS and/or ERA5 extremes
[9, 10]. In grid cells that frequently exceed defined intensity thresholds (for example, the local
95th percentile), unpaved links in low‑lying areas are assigned an elevated probability of
temporary closure, reflecting evidence that such events drive recurrent local disruption in urban
transport networks [3].
Chronic stress (heat and impermeability):
Areas combining high land‑surface temperature and high impermeable cover are assumed to
experience faster pavement degradation and higher susceptibility to flood damage [4]. Segments
in these areas are therefore given higher vulnerability weights, implemented as multipliers on
disruption probabilities.
These rules are applied directly to OSM highway and surface attributes, producing
climate‑adjusted network realisations for each scenario. Threshold values are aligned with ranges
reported in pluvial flood and transport-resilience studies [3, 8], and are further explored in the
sensitivity analysis.




3.3 Procedural Detail
The procedural workflow follows a systematic sequence designed to ensure reproducibility and
transparency:
   1.​ Download OSM network data for selected study cities using OSMnx [15], specifying
       network type (drive), bounding box coordinates, and retention of all road
       classifications.
   2.​ Clean and preprocess network data by removing disconnected components,
       standardizing attribute fields (road type, surface, length), and projecting to appropriate
       local coordinate reference systems.
   3.​ Calculate topological metrics (betweenness centrality, closeness centrality, node
       degree) for all network edges and nodes using NetworkX [16].
   4.​ Acquire climate datasets including CHIRPS precipitation data [9], ERA5 reanalysis
       [10], flood hazard maps [12], and Landsat/MODIS land surface temperature and land
       cover data [13, 14].
   5.​ Preprocess climate data by clipping to study area extents, resampling to a consistent
       spatial resolution, and calculating relevant indicators (e.g., 95th percentile rainfall
       intensity, mean annual flood depth, mean land surface temperature).
   6.​ Spatially join climate indicators to road network segments using geometric intersection
       or nearest-neighbor methods, assigning each segment relevant climate exposure values.
   7.​ Identify urban agriculture zones and markets from published sources [5, 6], field data,
       satellite imagery [13, 14], and OSM points of interest [17].
   8.​ Calculate origin-destination matrices between production zones and markets,
       identifying all feasible routes and calculating baseline travel times and distances.
   9.​ Apply climate-stress scenarios by implementing road-status rules (Section 3.2.1) to
       remove or downgrade vulnerable segments based on flood depth thresholds [3, 8],
       rainfall intensity exceedance [9, 10], and heat/impermeability indicators [4].
   10.​Recalculate network metrics and O-D matrices for each scenario to quantify changes in
       connectivity, travel time, route redundancy, and access.
   11.​Generate vulnerability indices by combining structural criticality scores with climate
       exposure indicators, weighted by supply-chain relevance [2, 8].
   12.​Conduct spatial analysis, including hotspot identification, scenario comparison, and
       supply-chain pathway mapping using GeoPandas and Matplotlib visualization tools.
   13.​Perform sensitivity analysis by varying threshold parameters (e.g., flood depth cutoffs,
       rainfall intensity percentiles) to assess robustness of findings.
   14.​Document and archive all code, data sources, parameter values, and intermediate
       outputs to ensure full reproducibility.




Results
Discussion:​
Assumptions and Limitations
Several assumptions and limitations shape the scope and interpretation of this study:
Uniform disruption thresholds: The analysis applies uniform flood depth and rainfall intensity
thresholds to determine road impassability (e.g., flood depth > 0.3 m for unpaved roads). These
thresholds are guided by ranges reported in pluvial flood impact studies on transport networks [3,
8], but actual thresholds vary with local drainage capacity, road construction quality, and
maintenance practices. Sensitivity analysis explores the impact of threshold variation, but
site-specific validation would strengthen the findings.
OSM data quality: OSM coverage and accuracy vary across regions, with some equatorial cities
having incomplete or outdated road network data [15, 17]. While OSM represents the best
available open-source option for many study areas, the analysis may underestimate network
extent or misclassify road types in areas with limited contributor activity. Where possible, OSM
data are cross-validated against satellite imagery and local sources.
Static network representation: The analysis treats road networks as static infrastructure, not
accounting for short-term adaptations such as temporary road closures, traffic management
interventions, or emergency rerouting by local authorities and users. Real-world supply chains
exhibit adaptive behaviors that may partially compensate for network disruptions, meaning the
study may overestimate actual impacts.
Focus on direct disruption: The methodology emphasizes direct transport network disruption
rather than full indirect economic impacts propagating through input-output linkages and
behavioral responses. While more complex integrated frameworks—such as the DisruptSCT
approach coupling network analysis with input-output and agent-based models [2]—capture
these indirect effects, they require extensive economic data, often unavailable for equatorial
cities. This study's focus on network-level vulnerability provides a necessary first step, but future
work should integrate economic impact modeling where data permit.
Climate scenario simplicity: Climate stress scenarios are based on historical climate data [9,
10] and global climate projections [11], which provide broad guidance but may not capture local
microclimatic variations or compound extreme events (e.g., simultaneous heat and flood stress).
The scenario design prioritizes transparency and replicability over exhaustive coverage of all
possible climate futures.
Urban agriculture data gaps: Data on urban agriculture production zones, market locations,
and supply chain flows are often incomplete or informal in equatorial cities [5, 6]. The study
relies on published sources, satellite imagery [13, 14], and proxy indicators, which introduce
uncertainty in identifying critical supply corridors. Participatory mapping and field validation
would improve spatial accuracy, but were beyond the scope of this desk-based analysis.
Generalizability: Although the methodology is designed for broad applicability across
equatorial regions, the specific findings reflect the characteristics of the selected study cities and
may not apply to all contexts. Differences in urban form, infrastructure quality, climate exposure,
and food system organization mean that vulnerability patterns and priority interventions will
vary. The open-source, reproducible approach facilitates adaptation and application to additional
cities.
Despite these limitations, the methodology offers a rigorous, transparent, and reproducible
framework for assessing the vulnerability of transport networks to climate stressors in urban
agriculture supply chains. The integration of open-source data, established network analysis
methods [15, 16], and climate science [3, 9, 10, 11] offers a practical tool for researchers and
practitioners working in data-scarce equatorial urban contexts.

Title & Abstract
Title:​
Assessment of Transport Networks Stability in the context of Climate Stressors on Urban
Agriculture Supply Chain in Equatorial regions.

Abstract:​
Urban agriculture contributes to food security, livelihoods, and environmental sustainability in
fast-growing urban areas, and its performance depends on reliable and stable transport networks
that connect farming sites with markets. In Northern Nigerian cities, variations in road conditions
and exposure to environmental factors influence accessibility and may affect agricultural
productivity and the movement of produce. This study builds on earlier analysis of Kano,
Kaduna, Sokoto, Maiduguri, and Jos by integrating transport network characteristics with
selected climate-related indicators to examine how environmental stressors interact with road
connections that support urban agriculture supply chains. The work applies a network-based
approach using OpenStreetMap data to identify farming sites, extract road geometries, and
compute travel distances between farms and central urban nodes. Spatial analysis in Python
enables the comparison of accessibility, network vulnerability, and climate-related pressures
across the study areas. The results highlight differences in network performance that have
implications for market integration, production efficiency, and urban planning. The study
provides a transferable geospatial procedure that can be applied in other equatorial regions to
support decision-making where formal datasets are limited.

Keywords:​
Equatorial regions, climate stressors, transport networks, network stability, spatial analysis,
urban agriculture, agricultural supply chains, Northern Nigeria




Research Objectives
   1.​ To evaluate approaches for assessing how urban agriculture in Northern Nigerian cities is
       impacted by transport network stability under climate stressors and their applicability to
       other Equatorial regions.
   2.​ To assess the performance of urban agriculture supply chains in Northern Nigerian cities
       with respect to market accessibility, network vulnerability, and transportation
       infrastructure, with implications for similar Equatorial regions.



   3.​ To highlight findings that are relevant to other Equatorial regions and to identify the main
       opportunities and challenges that transport network instability and climate hazards
       present to urban agriculture in Northern Nigerian cities.




Literature Review
Literature directly appraising urban agriculture's effectiveness from the perspective of transport
access is notably scarce. The articles under scrutiny, nonetheless, offer useful information on
transportation-related access measures affecting Northern Nigeria's urban agriculture
productivity. Based on the researched literature, Table 1 below gives an evaluation metric
system. Different research underscore the link between transport systems and agricultural trade,
stressing how modern road and rail networks lower travel time and therefore enlarge market
reach, especially in cattle commerce [7]. Lowering of transportation fees goes a long way to
increase food crop output efficiency [3]. Notwithstanding, high transport expenses and poor
infrastructure remain common and greatly slow down the efficiency of agricultural trade in
Nigeria, particularly in rural regions. Rising fuel prices, restricted vehicle supply, and bad road
conditions worsen logistical issues that make it expensive for farmers to move goods from rural
areas to urban markets [47]. Furthermore, the overdependence on road transportation along with
the absence of other logistics choices including railroads or waterways has stretched the
agricultural supply chain even more, increasing expenses and reducing the sector's
competitiveness. Development of cities, also known to raise transportation expenditures, is
another cause of lower urban agriculture efficiency. Dealing with these obstacles via better
transportation systems and cost-cutting measures is vital for increasing market reach and food
crop output efficiency.
